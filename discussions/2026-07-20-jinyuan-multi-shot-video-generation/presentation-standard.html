Literature report · 2026.07

This presentation examines multi-shot video generation. Rather than cataloguing papers one by one, it asks a shared question: how should multiple shots exchange information during generation? We organize the literature into four routes, from joint denoising in a single pass, to shot-by-shot generation, causal streaming, and finally planning and feedback systems outside the video model.

Our guiding view is that multi-shot generation is not merely the concatenation of short clips. A hard cut must introduce change while controlling which aspects of characters, props, environments, and narrative state persist and which are allowed to evolve.

We begin with the task definition. The input may be a complete story, or a global setup plus per-shot descriptions; the output is a video separated by explicit cuts. Viewpoint, composition, action, space, and time may change between shots, while the sequence still expresses a coherent narrative.

This is neither simply long-video generation nor multi-scene generation. The central challenge is consistency: because hard cuts inherently require change, evaluation cannot reward similarity alone. It must determine which aspects of character identity, prop state, environment, style, and causal structure should persist.

This slide serves as both the taxonomy and the table of contents. Holistic Joint allows all shots to interact bidirectionally within one call; Shot-Level AR invokes the model repeatedly, producing one complete shot each time; Causal Streaming reduces the generation unit further to a frame or chunk; and the System route plans, inspects, and corrects outside the video model.

The classification follows the primary inference interface, not the names of modules in individual papers. We now examine the four parts in sequence, then conclude with three slides comparing trade-offs, the benchmark landscape, and open questions.

The defining feature of Holistic Joint methods is that the number, boundaries, or durations of shots are fixed before inference, together with per-shot captions. An LLM may derive these captions automatically from the story, but they already exist when the video model is invoked. All visual tokens interact bidirectionally within the same denoising process, avoiding round-by-round rollout error accumulation.

The real challenge is that one context contains several captions: how can shot i primarily execute caption i while preserving visual communication across shots? This is the caption–shot alignment problem addressed in the next three slides.

The first family designs attention masks directly. Mask²DiT allows shot i to see only caption i, preventing text from leaking into the wrong shot. At the same time, visual tokens remain mutually visible across shots to preserve character and style consistency.

The second family modifies positional encoding instead of directly blocking attention. The difficulty is that local time may restart from zero in every shot: the model must represent both continuous time within a shot and the ordering of shots and their corresponding captions.

LCT assigns interleaved text–video pairs to non-overlapping positional intervals; EchoShot explicitly separates shot-aware and text-aware RoPE; MultiShotMaster marks shot boundaries with fixed phase discontinuities and binds reference tokens to specific shots and spatial regions. All three use positional geometry to represent identity and boundaries.

The third family uses transition tokens. ShotAdapter inserts K−1 copies of a shared token for K shots, giving the model an explicit control handle for “cut here.” The division of labor matters: duration and the local mask determine where the cut occurs, while the transition token reinforces the cutting operation itself.

Ablations show that the model can still generate multi-shot video when transition tokens are removed and only the local mask remains. Thus, the token is not the sole source of multi-shot capability. Its value lies in making a cut a learnable and controllable signal, although it does not yet distinguish complex editing patterns such as shot–reverse-shot.

HoloCine does not introduce a fourth caption-alignment primitive; instead, it extends one-pass joint generation to longer durations. Its training data cover approximately 5, 15, and 60 seconds, with up to roughly 13 shots in the longest examples. These figures describe training coverage, not a hard inference limit.

The key mechanism is sparse inter-shot self-attention: modeling remains dense within a shot, while representative or summary tokens provide bidirectional communication across shots, avoiding the quadratic cost of dense attention over the full sequence. The method still requires a complete script, and fine-grained long-range state may be lost in summary-based communication.

The trend in Part I is not an endless subdivision of alignment operators, but the transformation of generation structure into a directorial interface. Shot-Level AR uses a complete shot as the autoregressive unit. In each round, it first selects or compresses information from completed history, generates the current shot with one full diffusion sampling process, and then writes the result back to memory.

Unlike holistic generation, the past is fixed once generated and cannot be revised by future content. Unlike causal streaming, attention may remain bidirectional within a shot, and output usually appears only after the entire sampling process completes. Progress therefore centers not on accumulating more history frames, but on how memory is organized, retrieved, updated, and verified.

OneStory's central contribution is to reformulate multi-shot generation as next-shot generation while allowing the current caption to select evidence from the entire history. Frame Selection queries historical latent frames with the caption, avoiding a mechanical reliance on only the latest shot; the Adaptive Conditioner then compresses the selected context according to importance.

The compressed memory tokens enter the generator alongside the current noise tokens, enabling global historical scope within a compact conditioning budget. Shot captions can be appended iteratively, but the method does not plan the story automatically, and any historical error may become incorrect evidence for later retrieval.

FilmWeaver's main insight is to separate two temporal scales. Shot Cache retrieves keyframes from all earlier shots according to the current prompt, supporting long-range recall of characters and backgrounds; Temporal Cache stores recent frames from the current shot, supporting motion continuity and shot extension.

Both caches condition the same diffusion model in context, covering text-to-video, new-shot, and shot-extension modes. This shows that cross-shot identity and within-shot motion should not be addressed by the same memory. However, “arbitrary length” describes interface extensibility, not drift-free infinite rollout.

Memento turns the question “is this memory useful?” into a supervised objective. It not only optimizes next-shot generation but also requires the retrieved memory to reconstruct the subject, so identity preservation no longer depends solely on implicit similarity.

The importance of this design lies in establishing an auxiliary verification task for memory quality. A natural next step is to extend such verifiability beyond subject appearance to prop states, character relations, spatial layout, and event outcomes.

A strict definition of Causal Streaming has three components: generation continues causally by frame or latent chunk within each shot; history is reused through bounded KV, latent state, or reference frames; and new prompts may arrive during generation without recomputing already emitted content.

Merely calling a model repeatedly to complete one whole shot at a time does not qualify. The advantages are low time to first frame, open-ended extension, and runtime interaction; the costs are that future content cannot revise the past and errors propagate through causal state.

ShotStream first fine-tunes a text-to-video model into a bidirectional next-shot teacher: sparse historical frames from earlier shots condition generation of a complete target shot. The teacher is then distilled into a four-step causal student, enabling chunk-wise generation within each shot.

CausalCine takes a different approach: through 2N packing and parallel teacher forcing, it directly learns a native multi-shot causal base rather than treating a next-shot teacher as the final prior. The paper uses a 14B backbone and reports 16 FPS on eight H200 GPUs, so the figure is not directly comparable to ShotStream's single-GPU result.

The System route does not prescribe how generation occurs inside the video model. Instead, it decides outside the model how to decompose the story, in what order to generate, which references to preserve, and when to retry or revise subsequent inputs. It may call a joint, shot-AR, or causal renderer.

We distinguish stages not by the number of agents or module names, but by whether actual generated results affect subsequent decisions. This yields three clear stages: advance planning with unidirectional execution; candidate selection and external state management during generation; and a closed loop in which visual feedback truly alters shot order, prompts, memory, or retry strategy.

The System timeline has three stages. Early systems such as VideoDirectorGPT, DreamFactory, and ViGoT complete most planning before generation, then execute a fixed sequence of steps; actual rendered images rarely return to the planning module.

VideoDirectorGPT is an early example of the LLM-as-Director paradigm. GPT-4 first decomposes text into scenes, layouts, entities, and consistency groups, after which Layout2Vid generates each segment. DreamFactory divides script, character, and keyframe design among several agent roles.

Their important contribution is to introduce structured intermediate representations from film production into video generation. Their shared limitation is equally clear: planning happens first and rendering afterward, while generated imagery rarely returns to the planning module. The system knows what it intends to generate, but does not revise the plan using evidence from the rendered video.

ViGoT is a useful training-free system baseline. GPT-4o first produces a five-field script for each shot; portrait or IP embeddings anchor characters; a keyframe is generated for every shot, followed by I2V generation with DynamiCrafter; adjacent latent transitions soften the boundaries.

VideoMemory centers on a Dynamic Memory Bank that stores characters, props, and backgrounds separately as image references and textual attributes. A Storyboard Agent plans the script and retrieves required entities before generating each shot; when state changes or no match exists, it creates or updates a reference.

This is better suited to long stories than simply reusing the first frame: a character can change clothes or age, and a prop can change while remaining recognizable. However, the system mainly maintains the references it intends to use. It does not verify whether the final video is actually correct or trigger local regeneration accordingly, so it remains state management rather than a complete visual-feedback loop.

The change in 2026: not more agent roles, but visual evidence entering the decision process.
Conceptual pipeline redrawn from GroundShot

GroundShot's key contribution is not adding more agents, but changing the order in which shots are generated. Rather than following story order mechanically, it first identifies, generates, and verifies the shot best suited to serve as an entity reference. Only a reliable reference is written to memory and used for other shots.

This makes actual imagery evidence for subsequent decisions. Compared with AniMaker's local candidate selection and VideoMemory's continuous state maintenance, GroundShot and contemporaneous closed-loop methods adjust shot order, state records, prompts, and retry strategies according to the rendered output. This marks a genuine phase transition for the System route.

There is no single SOTA: the task interface determines the appropriate architecture.

Choose architecture after fixing interaction and editing requirements

The unified comparison shows that no single route wins on every dimension. Holistic methods excel at global bidirectional coordination under a fixed script, but have high time to first frame and expensive local edits. Shot-AR supports extension and reuses a fully bidirectional generator, but is vulnerable to memory drift. Causal Streaming has the lowest latency, but continually accumulates error.

Agentic systems can reorder, inspect, and regenerate, yet their cost and verifier bias cannot be ignored. Before discussing SOTA, we must therefore fix whether the user requires online extension or local editing, and how many retries and external API calls are permitted.

Questions?

We conclude with three questions for discussion. First, where should holistic and causal approaches meet? Can causal generation preserve interactivity while periodic bidirectional repair restores global quality? Second, should memory ultimately store frames, latent patches, entity assets, or explicit world state—and how should prop and event outcomes be verified?

Third, once agentic systems allow candidates, retries, and external APIs, how can quality, latency, and cost be compared fairly? Broadly, 2025 focused on bringing multiple shots into one generative context; in 2026, the emphasis shifts to online extension, historical state management, and using visual results in subsequent decisions.
